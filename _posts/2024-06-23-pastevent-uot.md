        <div class="timeline-content">
          <h3 class="timeline-title">
            The Money Talk: Startup Fundraising Unfiltered
          </h3>

          <p>
            <b>Fundraise for milestones, not just because you need money.</b><br>
            Salman emphasized that investors want to understand what founders have accomplished, what they plan to achieve next, and how the funding will get them there. These milestones might include validating customer demand, developing a product, or making key hires. Progress does not always mean success: founders who can explain what went wrong, what they learned, and how they adjusted their plans can still build investor confidence.
          </p>

          <p>
            <b>The right funding strategy depends on your business, not just its stage.</b><br>
            Canada offers several sources of capital, from government grants and non-dilutive funding to angel investment, venture capital, and venture debt. Kate encouraged founders to explore programs such as SR&amp;ED and IRAP before giving up equity unnecessarily. However, avoiding dilution is not always the best decision if it significantly delays commercialization. Founders should consider their industry, growth ambitions, and financial needs when deciding how much to raise and from whom.
          </p>

          <p>
            <b>A strong pitch builds credibility, not just excitement.</b><br>
            Salman shared that investors look for founders who deeply understand the problems they are solving and can demonstrate progress through concrete milestones. Honesty about setbacks is particularly important, as investors are evaluating the founding team as much as the business itself. Kate added that even specialized investors may not fully understand the technical details of a product. Founders should communicate the problem, solution, team, and go-to-market strategy clearly without overwhelming investors or disclosing confidential intellectual property.
          </p>

          <p>
            <b>The biggest cheque isn't always the best deal.</b><br>
            Kate highlighted several red flags in early-stage financing, including excessive equity demands, premature board seats, and investors offering advice or introductions in exchange for substantial ownership without providing capital. She also cautioned founders against rushing to sign term sheets under pressure. Financing decisions made at the pre-seed stage can affect every subsequent round, so founders need to understand what they are agreeing to. An investor who provides relevant expertise, customer introductions, and long-term support may be more valuable than one offering a larger cheque.
          </p>

          <p>
            <b>Fundraising starts long before the pitch.</b><br>
            Both speakers emphasized the importance of building relationships with investors before actively raising capital. Canada's VC ecosystem is closely connected, and warm introductions can be far more effective than cold outreach. Kate encouraged founders to connect with analysts and associates at investment firms, not just senior partners. Salman noted that investors often follow a company's progress over several months, using regular conversations to assess how the team handles challenges and delivers on milestones. These relationships help build trust before a formal investment decision.
          </p>

          <p>
            <b>Due diligence goes both ways.</b><br>
            Salman offered a look behind the scenes of investment decisions: after a pitch, investors assess whether they believe the founder, whether the team can execute its strategy, and whether the opportunity fits their fund. They may also contact customers, advisors, and other investors to validate the company's claims. Kate reminded founders that they should be evaluating investors just as carefully. Speaking with founders from an investor's existing portfolio can reveal how they work with companies, particularly during difficult periods. Since these relationships can last over a decade, compatibility matters.
          </p>

          <p>
            <b>AI is raising the bar for differentiation.</b><br>
            Looking ahead, both speakers discussed how AI is changing the startup landscape. With AI making it easier to develop products and prepare business materials, investors increasingly want to understand what makes a company genuinely different and defensible. Salman cautioned founders against relying on generic AI-generated pitches instead of demonstrating their own understanding of the business. Kate also highlighted growing interest in keeping Canadian technology companies in Canada, alongside opportunities in climate technology, dual-use innovation, and growth-stage investment.
          </p>

          <p>
            The audience Q&amp;A brought the conversation back to practical challenges faced by first-time founders, including negotiating investment terms, managing legal costs, validating market potential, and building a network from scratch. Kate encouraged students to approach networking with curiosity, ask genuine questions, and focus on building meaningful relationships rather than immediately seeking opportunities.
          </p>

          <p>
            <b>The discussion closed with a reminder that fundraising is more than securing capital: it's about demonstrating progress, earning trust, and choosing partners who can help build the company over the long term.</b>
          </p>

          <h3 class="timeline-title">
            Building in the Age of AI: The Founder's Playbook
          </h3>

          <p>
            <b>AI can accelerate the work, but context still matters.</b><br>
            While AI has lowered barriers to prototyping, data analysis, and increasingly business development, Warren emphasized that building successful solutions is still difficult. The challenge is often less about accessing the technology and more about understanding the specific context, workflows, data, users, and regulations into which it needs to fit. His shorthand: <b>context + competency builds confidence, which ultimately builds trust and adoption.</b>
          </p>

          <p>
            <b>Don’t outsource critical thinking.</b><br>
            Nardin highlighted one capability we should be especially careful not to delegate to AI: our own thinking and problem-solving. AI can make many tasks faster and easier, but founders still need to deeply understand the problem they are trying to solve rather than starting with a technology and searching for a use case. The process of working through difficult problems also builds skills that can easily be lost when too much is automated.
          </p>

          <p>
            Warren echoed this through the “4 Ds” of automation: tasks that are <b>dull, dangerous, or dirty</b> are natural candidates for automation, but <b>difficult</b> work should continue to challenge us. Novel and uncomfortable problems are often where human learning and value creation happen.
          </p>

          <p>
            <b>In healthcare, AI should augment rather than replace human expertise.</b><br>
            Nardin discussed AI’s potential to support diagnosis, medical imaging, documentation, research, and the analysis of large datasets. At the same time, healthcare depends on context, empathy, and understanding the impact of illness on an individual patient, qualities that cannot simply be automated. Human oversight and accountability remain essential, particularly when decisions affect patients.
          </p>

          <p>
            <b>Responsible adoption goes beyond the model itself.</b><br>
            Both speakers returned repeatedly to trust, privacy, accountability, and change management. Giving employees access to AI tools is not the same as successfully integrating AI into an organization. Leaders still need to validate outputs, protect sensitive data, understand the implications of AI-assisted decisions, and remain accountable for the results.
          </p>

          <p>
            <b>The opportunity is shifting from experimentation to commercialization.</b><br>
            Looking ahead, Warren highlighted growing opportunities for Canadian AI companies as organizations, governments, and SMEs move from experimentation toward adoption. For founders, this means becoming market-ready quickly and combining technical capability with strong domain knowledge and an understanding of how customers actually work.
          </p>

          <p>
            The discussion closed with advice that extended beyond AI: <b>stay curious, be willing to remain uncomfortable, trust yourself, and don’t wait for perfection before moving forward.</b>
          </p>
